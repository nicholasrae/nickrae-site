Pilot AI Tools: A Directory I Built for Aviation Study

A place to compare tools by the training problem they solve, then check the details before adding another app or subscription.

I built Pilot AI Tools to make aviation software easier to compare. It brings AI study tools, radio trainers, planning apps, and other pilot resources into a directory organized around what you want to practice or get done.

My starting point is the same as it is with the workbooks: give a pilot a useful next step. A long list of apps is not much help if you cannot tell which one fits your training stage, device, or budget.

Start with the task, then find the tool

The directory covers EFBs and flight planning, AI checkride preparation, ATC radio practice, ground study, weather, logbooks, simulators, and instructor tools. Those categories serve different jobs. An oral-practice assistant and an EFB do not belong on the same shopping list simply because both use aviation terminology.

If your immediate problem is explaining an answer out loud, start with AI checkride-prep tools. If it is hearing a clearance and forming a reply, look at ATC radio-practice tools. A category narrows the search; it does not establish that every listing will suit you.

What a listing tells you

Listings bring together fields such as price, free-tier availability, platform, source links, and review dates. I want those details to help you decide what deserves a closer look, without opening a dozen unrelated tabs first.

Read the fields separately. A review date tells you when a listing was checked; it does not mean I personally tested every feature. A source may support the platform information while another detail remains unknown. A vendor's feature description is still a vendor claim.

Some fields are incomplete or unverified. Treat an unknown price or free tier as a question to resolve on the product's own site. Before paying, check the current plan, renewal terms, device requirements, and exactly what is included. Directory information can change after a review.

A practical way to use the directory

  1. Write down one need. “Practice Class D radio exchanges” is easier to evaluate than “find a better aviation app.” Connect it to your next lesson or a specific weak area.
  2. Check what you already have. Your school, ground course, or current subscription may cover the same job. Compare platform support before spending time on an app you cannot use.
  3. Read the original sources. Follow the listing's product links, confirm current terms, and look for an explanation of how the tool handles updates, references, and your data.
  4. Try a small, repeatable exercise. If a trial or free version is offered, use one known scenario. Can you understand the feedback, find its source, and explain the answer without the app?
  5. Review the result with your instructor. Keep the tool if it supports a useful practice routine. An attractive answer or a high practice score alone does not establish readiness.

For AI-generated explanations, checking the cited reference is part of the exercise. A fluent response can still be wrong or omit an important condition. Use study tools to prepare questions and practice reasoning; use current authoritative references, approved aircraft information, and instruction for the decisions they govern. The directory's resources page is another starting point for source material.

Keep the practice simple

You do not need an AI subscription to start answering scenarios out loud. If you are working toward a private-pilot checkride, try my five free PPL Oral Scenario Cards. Pick a card, explain your answer, check the cited source, and take any uncertainty to your instructor. Confirm your email to receive the PDF. I’ll also send occasional aviation study tips, and you can unsubscribe anytime.

A tool earns a place in the routine when it helps you do that work more consistently. That is the standard I want this directory to help you apply.
