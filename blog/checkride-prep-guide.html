Private Pilot Checkride Prep: ACS, Oral Study, and Checklist

Private pilot checkride prep with an ACS study map, oral-practice workflow, worked fuel example, and final-week checklist to review with your CFI.

Start with the ACS, then practice explaining decisions. Make a list of the tasks you cannot yet explain or demonstrate consistently, add the deficient areas on your knowledge-test report, and work through them with your aircraft documents and CFI. Use the final-week checklist if your test is close, or the example eight-week sequence to organize a longer preparation period.

This is a ground-study framework informed by my own checkride experience. It helps you organize preparation; it cannot determine readiness, replace flight instruction, or guarantee a pass. Which ACS is current?

On October 22, 2026, the FAA ACS index lists Private Pilot for Airplane Category, FAA-S-ACS-6C, effective May 31, 2024. Recheck the index before your test. This guide focuses on airplane preparation; work with your instructor to identify the tasks applicable to your class and application.

Turn the ACS into a study map

The ACS combines knowledge, risk management, and skills. A study plan needs all three: what the rule or system means, what could go wrong in the scenario, and what you must demonstrate. See the ACS introduction and Preflight Preparation tasks.

  1. Start with the actual standard. List each applicable Area of Operation and Task. Record the task code beside your notes so you can return to the complete requirement rather than a remembered question.
  2. Add your knowledge-test deficiencies. Match the codes on your Airman Knowledge Test Report (AKTR) to the ACS. The FAA ACS Companion Guide, Section 2, explains that the evaluator reviews the report and assesses the noted deficiencies during the oral. Your instructor's required endorsement also addresses satisfactory knowledge of those deficient subject areas under 14 CFR § 61.39(a)(6).
  3. Keep an evidence column. Beside each weak task, note the current regulation, FAA reference, or aircraft POH/AFM section you used. Write down unresolved questions for your CFI.
  4. Retest the explanation. Close your notes, explain the answer aloud, then change one assumption. A changed passenger load, forecast, or equipment status helps reveal whether you understand the connection.

For example, Cross-Country Flight Planning includes fuel requirements and reserves (PA.I.D.K3c), fuel-planning risk (PA.I.D.R6), and recalculating reserves for an evaluator-provided scenario (PA.I.D.S4). Those are three study prompts within one task, not three unrelated facts. Read the complete task in the ACS.

Practice an oral answer with sources

Use a repeatable ground-study sequence: answer, source, application, limitation. State your answer in plain language, locate its source, connect it to the airplane and scenario, and identify what information could change the answer. This is a suggested study method, not an FAA-prescribed script or a list of questions your examiner will ask.

Build a small set of connected prompts

  • Pilot qualifications: What documents, privileges, limitations, and recent experience apply to this proposed flight? Distinguish currency from proficiency. Start with ACS Task I.A.
  • Airworthiness and systems: Where are the aircraft's limitations and inspection records? How would you research an inoperative item in this specific airplane? Use Tasks I.B and I.G with the aircraft's documents.
  • Weather and planning: Which reports and forecasts matter to the route, and what would make you delay or change the plan? Connect Tasks I.C and I.D.
  • Performance and loading: Can you explain the inputs, chart notes, limitations, and uncertainty in your weight-and-balance and performance work? Use Task I.F and the applicable POH/AFM.
  • Human factors: How do fatigue, stress, distraction, or external pressure affect this scenario? Start with Task I.H.

These prompts follow ACS Area of Operation I; they are a starting set, not a substitute for reviewing every applicable task. If you cannot explain a point, record the gap and resolve it with the source and your instructor. Finding a reference does not replace the knowledge or performance the task requires.

Worked example: fuel arithmetic, rule, and margin

The desk exercise: For a 30 NM leg, assume a constant 100-knot groundspeed, 8 gallons of usable fuel at the start, and a constant 8 GPH burn at normal cruise. Ignore other fuel use for this calculation. What remains after the leg, and how does that compare with day and night VFR airplane reserves?

Show the calculation and comparison
  1. Time: 30 NM ÷ 100 knots = 0.3 hours, or 18 minutes.
  2. Fuel used: 0.3 hours × 8 GPH = 2.4 gallons.
  3. Fuel remaining: 8 − 2.4 = 5.6 gallons; 5.6 ÷ 8 × 60 = 42 minutes at the assumed burn.
  4. Comparison: 42 minutes is 12 minutes above the 30-minute day threshold and 3 minutes below the 45-minute night threshold.

Change one assumption: At 80 knots, the same leg takes 22.5 minutes and uses 3 gallons. The remaining 5 gallons represents 37.5 minutes at 8 GPH: 7.5 minutes above the day threshold and 7.5 minutes below the night threshold.

The rule: 14 CFR § 91.151(a) governs beginning a VFR airplane flight. Considering wind and forecast weather, it requires enough fuel to the first point of intended landing and, assuming normal cruising speed, at least another 30 minutes by day or 45 minutes at night.

The limitation: This comparison does not establish that a real flight or diversion is safe or legal. It omits taxi, climb, descent, routing changes, delays, and uncertainty in fuel quantity and burn. For study, explain which assumptions need verification in the aircraft documents and why a legal minimum is different from your planning margin. Review the complete fuel-planning task with your CFI; this is ground-study arithmetic, not a flight plan or in-flight instruction.

Review flight tasks with your CFI

Use the ACS to brief and debrief your training, with your CFI directing the flight practice. Review the applicable takeoff and landing, maneuver, navigation, basic instrument, emergency, and postflight tasks. This page does not teach their execution.

Use the standard for the specific task

A tolerance copied from one task does not become a standard for all flying. In the official ACS, compare:

  • Steep Turns — PA.V.A.S5: this element has its own entry-altitude, airspeed, bank, and rollout-heading limits.
  • Basic Instrument Straight-and-Level Flight — PA.VIII.A.S2: this element has different altitude, heading, and airspeed limits.
  • Recovery from Unusual Flight Attitudes — PA.VIII.E.S1–S2: these elements address identification, recovery, and resource management; they do not establish a generic ±200-foot recovery allowance.

For each task, bring the full knowledge, risk, and skill elements into the debrief. Record the specific gap and your instructor's next training objective. A checklist of maneuvers completed once is not a readiness assessment.

Connect risk to the same scenario

Use PAVE as four prompts: Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, External pressures. For each, identify a hazard, its possible consequence, and what would change the plan. The Cross-Country Flight Planning task explicitly includes these four risk categories, plus fuel planning, ATC-service limitations, and EFB use where applicable.

  • Pilot: What would fatigue or a gap in proficiency change?
  • Aircraft: Which limitation or unresolved maintenance question matters to this plan?
  • Environment: Which weather, airport, terrain, or airspace condition reduces your margin?
  • External pressures: What changes if a passenger needs to arrive at a fixed time?

State your personal minimums and discuss their reasoning with your CFI. The goal of this exercise is to connect the hazard to a decision, rather than simply name a checklist.

An adaptable eight-week study sequence

This is an example sequence, not an FAA requirement or a promise of readiness in eight weeks. Adjust it with your CFI to your training record, weak areas, aircraft availability, and test date.

Weeks 8–6: Build the map

  • Review your knowledge-test status and training record with your instructor.
  • List the applicable ACS tasks and mark weak knowledge, risk, and skill elements.
  • Organize current FAA references and the documents for your test airplane.

Weeks 5–3: Apply and explain

  • Practice oral answers aloud using one connected flight-planning scenario.
  • Change one assumption and repeat the reasoning.
  • Bring unresolved questions and flight debrief notes to your CFI; update the weak-area list.

Weeks 2–1: Review readiness and logistics

  • Run a practice oral with your instructor and revisit every unresolved deficiency.
  • Review applicable flight-task standards, aircraft limitations, and emergency checklists with your CFI.
  • Complete the document review and final-week checklist below.

Private pilot checkride week checklist

Use this as a discussion list with your instructor and examiner. The ACS Appendix 1 covers test roles and outcomes; § 61.39 covers practical-test prerequisites. Your application may require additional items.

  • Application and eligibility: review IACRA/application status, required endorsements, identification, pilot documents, applicable medical qualification, training records, and knowledge-test report with your CFI.
  • Knowledge-test deficiencies: resolve the subject areas noted on your AKTR and confirm the required instructor endorsement.
  • ACS map: revisit each applicable task and the knowledge, risk, or skill gaps you have recorded.
  • Aircraft: organize required documents and the references for maintenance status, inspections, limitations, systems, loading, and performance.
  • Assigned scenario: review current weather, charts, NOTAMs, TFRs, fuel, loading, performance, alternatives, and your go/no-go reasoning. Connect the work to ACS Tasks I.B–I.G and the applicable § 91.103 preflight requirements.
  • Tools: know how to use the installed avionics and any portable tools in your plan, what their information cannot tell you, and what backup you would use. Review applicable ACS equipment and EFB elements with your CFI.
  • Final briefing: confirm the appointment, assigned planning work, documents, and test logistics with the examiner. Discuss discontinuance and what happens if weather, aircraft status, or readiness changes.
What I would repeat: practice rule, risk, and action together. A correct number is useful; explaining what it means for this airplane, this weather, and this scenario makes the study more useful.

Keep the study focused

Choose the next unresolved ACS task, find the source, explain the application, and take the remaining gap to your instructor. That gives each study session a concrete purpose without turning a practice score or a memorized answer into a promise of readiness.
