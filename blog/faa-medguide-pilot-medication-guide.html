FAA MedGuide: What Pilots Should Know

What the FAA’s medication reference can help you find, the limits of an “allowed” listing, and questions to take to your AME and treating clinician.

Educational resource note, not medical advice or an individual fitness-to-fly determination. Sources reviewed October 22, 2026.

A medication lookup can help you find the right question to ask. It cannot answer every question about whether you should fly. I think the FAA’s MedGuide is worth bookmarking, provided you keep that distinction in view.

What the guide covers

The FAA’s MedGuide supports airman medical certification and FAA controller clearance. Version 1.0 currently lists only allowed medications; conditional and unacceptable categories are planned. A missing entry therefore does not establish approval or rejection. “Allowed” depends on a stable, aeromedically acceptable condition and no adverse effects. Aeromedical determinations still require individualized assessment; the guide is not clinical advice for diagnosis or treatment.

Read the entry, not just the label

The guide distinguishes initial, subsequent-dose and discontinuation wait times. Its usual 48-hour initial observation period has exceptions; medication-specific guidance and the condition can require different or longer observation. There is no universal 48-hour clearance rule. Read the FAA’s definitions and instructions alongside the entry.

My preference is to approach the lookup as preparation for a conversation. Write down what the entry actually says, what it leaves unanswered, and which question needs a qualified person’s judgment. A search result, a screenshot or a short summary can lose the conditions attached to the original entry.

Two questions belong in the same conversation

The FAA’s medication guidance for pilots says both the treatment and the underlying condition can affect flight safety. Its references do not replace consultation with your treating clinician and Aviation Medical Examiner (AME). It also cautions that a primary care physician may not be trained in aviation medicine.

That makes the division of responsibility worth discussing explicitly: your clinician manages your care, while an AME can help address its aviation implications. Do not start, stop or alter treatment on the strength of this article or a directory entry. Bring individual medication and return-to-flight questions to those professionals.

Keep the operating rule in view

14 CFR § 61.53 addresses operations during medical deficiency. Its requirements differ according to the operation and medical-certificate or driver’s-license provisions. Holding a current medical certificate does not remove the separate responsibility described there. Read the branch that applies to your operation; a medication listing does not replace it.

The FAA’s Medications and Flying brochure also warns that impairment can occur even when someone feels alert. Feeling normal is not, by itself, a reliable clearance test.

A useful next step

I would keep a short reference note rather than build a personal “approved drugs” list:

  • Bookmark the official page so you can return to the current instructions.
  • Separate the entry’s wording from your own interpretation.
  • Put unresolved questions in a list for your AME and treating clinician.
  • Recheck the source when revisiting an old note rather than assuming it still applies.

This is the same reading habit I want to encourage throughout Pilot Notes: find the source, notice its limits, and know when the next step is a conversation rather than another search.
