Is a 35-Minute VFR Fuel Reserve Enough?

A daytime plan leaves five minutes above the legal minimum. Work through the oral question and decide what would make you change the plan.

The oral question

You’re planning a daytime VFR flight in a piston airplane. You’ve accounted for forecast winds, and the plan leaves enough fuel after your first intended landing for 35 minutes at normal cruise. Your passenger wants to leave now. What would you tell them?

What the rule requires

Before starting a VFR airplane flight under Part 91, account for winds and forecast weather. You need enough fuel to reach your first intended landing and then fly at normal cruising speed for at least 30 minutes by day or 45 minutes at night. The 35-minute daytime plan clears that fuel minimum by five minutes. For the same plan at night, it would be 10 minutes short. 14 CFR § 91.151(a)

What happens when the forecast changes?

Suppose a new forecast before departure adds 10 minutes to the trip at the same cruise fuel flow, with every other assumption held constant. Say the new reserve out loud before opening the answer.

Show the reserve calculation

35 planned minutes minus 10 additional minutes leaves 25 minutes at the same fuel flow. The revised plan falls five minutes below the day VFR airplane minimum. Replan before departing.

On a real flight, use the applicable AFM or POH for fuel calculations and verify the usable fuel on board. Account for fuel used during taxi, climb and other phases at the appropriate rates. A longer route, higher fuel burn or a delay near the destination can all reduce the arrival reserve.

Make the decision before the passenger asks

  1. Set your personal reserve before the flight. Use today’s conditions, your aircraft and the available fuel stops to choose a more conservative margin with your instructor.
  2. Pick an early decision point. Choose a checkpoint to compare actual progress with your plan and update the arrival reserve. Identify a suitable fuel stop to use if the estimate drops below your planning margin.
  3. Change the plan while you have options. When the margin is inadequate, consider adding fuel within weight-and-balance limits, shortening the leg, stopping for fuel or postponing the flight. Explain the change to your passenger before leaving.

For the checkride, practice saying the regulation, the risk to the margin and your next action in your own words. The Private Pilot ACS tests calculating fuel requirements, managing external pressure and recalculating reserves when the scenario changes. FAA Private Pilot Airplane ACS, Area I, Task D
