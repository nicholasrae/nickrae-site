The right fit

What you’ll work on.

For pilots adding multi-engine privileges, especially those training in a PA-44 Seminole environment. It is intended for ground study and oral preparation alongside the flight training required for the add-on.

PA-44-focused multi-engine study. Aircraft-specific speeds, limitations, procedures and performance must come from your actual POH/AFM, approved checklists and instructor; a study workbook cannot set them for your aircraft.

  1. 01

    V-speeds and systems

    Organize the speeds and systems you need to explain, and connect them to your actual airplane’s documents.

  2. 02

    Vmc and asymmetric thrust

    Review the concepts and factors discussed during multi-engine training without treating a demonstration as an unsupervised exercise.

  3. 03

    One-engine-inoperative operations

    Ground-study prompts for engine-out procedures, single-engine decisions and OEI approaches.

From the review page

Explain your answer out loud.

Source page 111 contains questions 3–7 from a longer exercise. The selected page preserves those five prompts; the sample does not add model answers or reproduce the intervening pages.

  1. During the practical, you identify and feather the wrong engine. What do you say to the DPE?
  2. Vmc increases with altitude. True or false? Explain.
  3. You pass your checkride. Can you now legally fly a multi-engine airplane IFR? Under what conditions?
  4. The DPE asks whether the Seminole has a critical engine. What is the correct answer and why?
  5. In OEI flight, is the ball centered the correct condition for best climb performance? Explain zero sideslip.

These are discussion questions, not statements to memorize or in-flight instructions. Resolve your answers with the current applicable ACS, FAA references, actual aircraft POH/AFM and instructor.

Chapter 12: Checkride Day

You made it. Now do not blow it on a paperwork problem or a self-inflicted verbal disaster. This chapter covers what to bring, how to survive the oral, how to fly the practical, and what to do when things go sideways. They will go sideways. That is fine.

What to Bring

Show up with everything. Missing one document ends the checkride before it starts.

Pilot certificate (current)

Current medical certificate (appropriate class)

Government-issued photo ID

Logbook with all required flight time and the multi-engine training logged

Instructor endorsements: both the training endorsement and the solo endorsement if applicable

Completed FAA Form 8710-1 or IACRA application (reviewed and signed by your CFI)

Aircraft logbooks: airframe, engine(s), prop(s)

Current weight and balance

Current equipment list

POH for the specific aircraft you are flying

Maintenance records showing the aircraft is airworthy

Put this list on a sticky note. Check it the night before. Check it again in the morning. A forgotten logbook is a wasted day and a wasted examiner fee. That is the standard.

The Oral Exam: How DPEs Think

The DPE is not your enemy. They are not hunting for a reason to fail you. They are calibrating one thing: are you safe to fly a multi-engine airplane without supervision?

They ask questions to find where your knowledge ends. That is their job. When they find the edge, they stop. Your job is to make that edge as far out as possible.

Answer what is asked. Nothing more. If the question is “What is Vmc?” answer that. Do not volunteer the nine factors, the critical engine, and your thoughts on propeller aerodynamics unless asked. Every extra sentence is an invitation for a follow-up question you may not be able to answer.

Be direct. Be confident. Be brief.

One useful study session

Give the question some work.

  1. Put the correct POH beside the workbook

    Identify the airplane and document revision used in your training. Mark where an answer needs aircraft-specific information.

  2. Explain before rehearsing

    Discuss systems and performance on the ground. Learn and practice actual procedures through your instructor’s training plan.

  3. Debrief the differences

    Write down what changed with conditions or configuration and which source settles the answer. Use those notes in your next oral-review session.

Keep the current sources beside you.

Use this workbook with your instructor, the applicable FAA Airman Certification Standards, current FAA handbooks and your aircraft’s POH/AFM. Published study examples do not replace current regulations, approved procedures or actual flight instruction. No FAA endorsement or checkride result is promised.
